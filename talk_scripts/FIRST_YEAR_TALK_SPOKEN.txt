So, how did humanity get from zero to that?

AI often feels like it fell out of the sky in 2022. In reality, it is an 80-year story of brilliant ideas, overblown promises, funding crashes, and stubborn comebacks. The yellow line we're about to trace isn't a strict measurement - think of it as the emotional and financial temperature of AI: hype and funding!

Right here at the start, in 1943, neurophysiologist Warren McCulloch and teenage logician Walter Pitts wrote down the first mathematical sketch of a brain cell - an artificial neuron with inputs, weights, and a firing threshold.

In 1950, Alan Turing famously asked, "Can machines think?" In 1956, a summer workshop at Dartmouth College coined the phrase Artificial Intelligence. And in 1958, Frank Rosenblatt wired up the Perceptron - a physical cabinet of motors and dials that could actually learn its own weights from examples to tell simple shapes apart! We'll play with a live Perceptron in Chapter 4. Newspapers went wild; Rosenblatt predicted machines would soon walk, talk, and be conscious.

Then came the reality check. In 1969, Marvin Minsky and Seymour Papert published a book proving mathematically that a single layer of Perceptrons couldn't even solve tiny logic puzzles, and nobody knew a practical way to train multiple layers yet. In 1973, the UK's Lighthill Report declared AI had failed to deliver on its grand promises. Governments slashed research budgets overnight. That icy blue band is the First AI Winter.

In the 1980s, hype roared back around hand-coded expert systems for corporations. Meanwhile, quietly in 1986, Rumelhart, Geoffrey Hinton, and Williams popularised backpropagation - the mathematical recipe that finally let multi-layer neural networks learn from their mistakes! Backpropagation is the star of Chapter 5. By 1989, Yann LeCun used it so post offices could automatically read handwritten zip codes on envelopes. Yet computers were still millions of times too slow, hand-coded expert systems proved brittle and expensive to maintain, and by the late eighties the field froze into a Second AI Winter. For years, AI was practically a taboo word on grant applications!

Through the nineties and 2000s, patient researchers rebuilt the foundations. In 1997, IBM's Deep Blue beat world chess champion Garry Kasparov - though by brute-force board search, not neural networks! Then, in 2009, Fei-Fei Li and colleagues assembled ImageNet: 14 million internet photos painstakingly labelled by hand. For the first time, AI had enough rich data to study from.

And in 2012, lightning struck. Two grad students and Geoffrey Hinton trained a deep neural network called AlexNet on two video-game graphics cards - GPUs - and obliterated the ImageNet competition, cutting the error rate nearly in half overnight, from 26.2 percent down to 15.3 percent. Deep learning was suddenly undeniable. Four years later, in 2016, DeepMind's AlphaGo defeated legendary champion Lee Sedol 4 to 1 at Go - a board game with more possible positions than atoms in the observable universe.

Then in June 2017, eight researchers at Google published a landmark paper titled "Attention Is All You Need," inventing the Transformer - an architecture that could process whole pages of words in parallel instead of reading painfully word-by-word. We'll see how Attention works in Chapter 6. By 2020, scaling up Transformers gave us OpenAI's GPT-3 in language, while DeepMind's AlphaFold 2 cracked the 50-year grand challenge of predicting 3D protein structures.

In November 2022, ChatGPT put a chat box in front of a language model and brought AI to hundreds of millions of students and workers overnight. Google launched Gemini 1.0 natively across text, images, and audio in 2023; in 2024, AI pioneers won two Nobel Prizes; reasoning models arrived in 2025; and today we sit at Gemini 4 Argon.

Notice something wild about that timeline: neurons are from 1943, and backpropagation is from 1986. Why didn't this happen forty years ago?

In AI, you'll constantly hear the word tensor - as in Google's TensorFlow library or Tensor Processing Units. Tensor sounds like intimidating third-year physics, so let's demystify it in thirty seconds using the animation on the left and the table on the right.

One lone number - say, today's temperature, 22.5 - is called a scalar.
Stack numbers into a 1D list - say, 640 numbers describing one word - and mathematicians call it a vector.
Arrange numbers into a 2D flat grid of rows and columns - like an Excel sheet - and we call it a matrix.
Stack three 2D grids behind one another - Red, Green, and Blue for Grace Hopper's photo - and you have a 3D tensor.
Put 32 colour photos into a folder together to process all at once, and you have a 4D tensor!

That's all a tensor is: a neatly organised box of numbers with as many dimensions as you need. And a neural network is simply a machine where tensors flow in one end, get transformed step by step, and flow out the other - literally Tensor-Flow!

So what is the tiniest building block that transforms those numbers? Let's watch a 16-second animation of one artificial neuron.

Which brings us to Chapter 5 - the million-dollar question: How on earth do you automatically tune 268 million knobs from random noise into intelligence?

All machine learning - from fitting a line in first-year physics to training Gemini 4 - comes down to a three-step recipe.

Step 1: Invent a single number that measures how wrong your current knobs are. We call that number the Loss, written as a red capital L. If L is huge, your AI is terrible. If L is close to zero, your AI is spot-on.

Let's watch a machine rediscover a famous law of the solar system purely by shrinking L! On the left are the eight planets, from Mercury out to Neptune. On the horizontal axis x is distance from the Sun, on a log base 10 scale. On the vertical axis y is how long it takes the planet to orbit the Sun once, also log base 10.

Our tiny model is a straight line with just two knobs: slope w and intercept b. Right now, with w equal to 0.60 and b equal to 0.40, our yellow guess line misses almost every planet! Look at the vertical red bars connecting each planet to our line: that vertical gap is the prediction error, prediction minus truth. Why do we square each error in the equation on the right? Two reasons: squaring makes negative misses positive so errors don't cancel each other out, and it punishes giant blunders heavily! Here, each squared error is literally drawn as a red geometrical square, and our Loss, L equals 0.4494, is simply the average area of those eight red squares.

Watch what happens if I grab one of the yellow handles on the line.

Learning means making the red squares vanish! Let's jump to the mathematical minimum.

Look at that: the red squares disappear as L drops to roughly zero, the intercept b hits 0.00, and the slope w lands on 1.50 - three-halves! Remember from logarithms that log T equals three-halves log a means T squared is proportional to a cubed. In 1619, Johannes Kepler spent years of gruelling hand arithmetic on Tycho Brahe's star tables to discover Kepler's Third Law of Planetary Motion. Our two-knob machine just rediscovered it in a millisecond by minimising a Loss number!

And when we switch back from logarithmic axes to normal distance in Astronomical Units versus Earth years, our straight line becomes that gorgeous astronomical curve sweeping cleanly through Mercury, Earth, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune!

Of course, with 2 knobs we could eyeball the best line - with 268 million knobs, you're flying blind. How does the computer find the bottom of L automatically?

Imagine standing blindfolded on a foggy mountain at night, trying to reach the lowest valley. What do you do? You feel the slope under your feet and take a step downhill!

In calculus, the slope is the derivative d-L over d-w, and our step-size knob is orange eta, the learning rate.
Watch the orange ball first when eta is too big: it leaps right across the valley and bounces wildly!
Now watch when eta is just right: as the valley flattens out, the steps automatically get smaller and gentler until the ball settles right into the minimum. That algorithm is called Gradient Descent.

Let's try Learning Step 2, Gradient Descent, live on our Kepler planet problem!

Look at the colorful contour map on the left. This is the Loss Landscape. The horizontal axis is every possible choice of slope w. The vertical axis is every possible choice of intercept b. Colour shows how wrong that w and b pair is: dark navy blue around the edges is high error, the mountain peaks, and that bright golden bullseye around w equals 1.50, b equals 0.00 is the valley floor where Kepler's Law lives!

Our orange ball starts up on the blue hillside at w equals 0.2, b equals 1.2. That glowing green arrow is the negative gradient - pointing straight down the steepest slope from where the ball is standing right now. Look at the update equation on the top right: New w equals old w minus learning-rate eta times the slope. Why minus? Because the slope points uphill, so subtracting it steps downhill!

Let's let the ball roll with a sensible learning rate, eta equals 0.10.

Look at the three panels working together: on the map, the ball curves gracefully down the valley into 1.498 comma zero; on the middle-right mini-plot, the yellow line locks onto the eight planets; and on the bottom-right graph, the red Loss plunges down four orders of magnitude!

Now, you might wonder: "Why take 180 tiny steps? Why not crank the learning rate eta up so it finishes faster?" Let's try eta equals 0.60.

Whoa! It gets there in 27 steps, alright - by slamming back and forth off opposite canyon walls like a pinball! What if we push our luck just a tiny bit further, to eta equals 0.80?

Kaboom - diverged! Every step overshoots onto a higher part of the opposite wall, where the slope is even steeper, flinging the ball into outer space and sending the Loss to infinity! Choosing the right learning rate - and using smart modern optimisers like Adam that adapt step sizes automatically - is one of the biggest practical crafts in training real AI.

In our Kepler example, we only had 2 knobs, so we could draw the Loss Landscape as a 3D terrain of hills and valleys. And real neural network landscapes aren't simple bowls; they have ridges, saddle points, and shallow side-pockets called local minima that momentum helps the ball swing right through, just like in this animation.

Now stretch your imagination: Gemma 3 270-million has 268 million knobs. That means its Loss Landscape is a mountain range in 268-million-dimensional space!

Neither you nor I can picture 268 million dimensions - three dimensions already gives most of us a headache! Here is the miracle of calculus: Gradient Descent doesn't need to visualise the landscape. It just needs a list of 268 million slopes - one slope for each knob, telling that knob "turn slightly up" or "turn slightly down."

Wait a second, though. How do you calculate 268 million slopes without testing each knob one at a time, which would take thousands of years? That brings us to Learning Step 3 - the 1986 breakthrough that won Geoffrey Hinton a Nobel Prize.

Meet Backpropagation - short for backward propagation of errors, or simply assigning the blame.
First, in the forward pass, numbers flow left to right through each operation box to make a prediction, y-hat equals 0.731, and measure the Loss, L equals 0.0723.
Then, in the backward pass, green pulses carry blame right to left, multiplying each box's simple local slope together!
If you've seen the Chain Rule in high-school or first-year calculus - if w nudges z, z nudges y-hat, and y-hat nudges L, you just multiply the three sensitivities together - that is all backpropagation is!

Let's walk through that exact blame game step-by-step so you see why it's so fast.

Here is a tiny one-neuron assembly line: input x equals 2.0, starting weight w equals 0.500, bias b equals 0, and we want the network to output y equals 1.0.

Pass 1: Forward, following the blue pulses.
2.0 times 0.500 gives 1.00.
Plus 0 gives z equals 1.00.
Sigmoid squashes 1.00 into our prediction, y-hat equals 0.731 - too low, since we wanted 1.0!
Squared error, 0.731 minus 1.0, all squared, gives Loss L equals 0.0723.

Now: how should we adjust yellow w? Watch the green pulses flow backward from L.

Pass 2: Backward, following the green pulses. Look at the magic here: nobody in this chain needs to understand the whole network! Each little circle only knows its own tiny, local rule, and passes the multiplied message one step left.
The Loss box says: "Hey y-hat, if you went up, the error would go down by negative 0.538."
The Sigmoid box multiplies by its own curve slope, 0.197, and passes the message to the addition node.
The Multiply box multiplies by x, which is 2.0, and hands yellow w its exact gradient: the slope of L with respect to w equals negative 0.2115! Because that slope is negative, increasing w will shrink the error!

Step 3: Nudge the weights! Weight w steps uphill from 0.500 to 0.712, and look at the Loss box on the right - L immediately drops in half from 0.0723 to 0.0366! Now let's repeat that forward, backward, nudge loop 20 times in a row.

Watch w climb past 1.4, watch y-hat climb past 0.95 toward 1.0, and look at that red Loss sparkline in the top-right corner - plummeting toward zero!

Because of the Chain Rule, one forward pass plus one backward pass gives you the exact slope for all 268 million weights in a single sweep - taking roughly only twice as much work as running the model forward!

How do we go from classifying 2D dots to chatting in English in the Gemini Web App?

Earlier we saw that a tokenizer gives every word a dictionary ID number - like "Jupiter" is number 52,895 and "Saturn" is number 56,949. But an ID number by itself is arbitrary, like your student ID number: two students whose ID numbers are next to each other aren't necessarily best friends!

So the very first table inside a language model turns each token ID into a learned vector of 640 decimal numbers - called a word embedding. Think of those 640 numbers as GPS coordinates in a 640-dimensional Map of Meaning. During training, backpropagation nudges those coordinates too, so words that appear in similar contexts naturally drift toward the same neighbourhood!

On the left, we took around 250 real words out of Gemma 3's 640-dimensional map and squashed them onto a flat 2D screen using a technique called t-SNE. Nobody ever gave Gemma a science textbook list of categories - yet look at the clusters it organised all by itself: planets and space in yellow, chemical elements in teal, animals in green, emotions in pink, sciences in purple!

Let's look up "Jupiter" across all 262,144 tokens in Gemma's entire vocabulary.

Look at the right-hand readout for Jupiter: out of a quarter of a million tokens, its closest neighbours in 640-dimensional space are Saturn, Venus, Neptune, Mars, Titan, Pluto, Uranus, and Zeus - its twin king of the gods from Greek mythology!

Even wilder, look at the Vector Arithmetic box on the bottom right. Because directions in this map capture relationships, you can literally add and subtract words like coordinates!
Take the 640 numbers for king, subtract man, add woman, and the closest word in Gemma's vocabulary is queen!
Paris, minus France, plus Japan lands on Tokyo.
Sodium, minus N-a, plus K lands on potassium!
And in the spirit of scientific honesty, we left two real misses in red at the bottom too - in this tiny 270-million-parameter model, A-u minus gold plus silver guessed C-u, copper, instead of A-g!

Still, a word's dictionary coordinate is fixed - "bank" has the same starting coordinates whether you mean a river bank or a Commonwealth Bank! How does a model figure out what words mean in context? That brings us to Google's 2017 superpower: Attention.

Read this sentence: "The wombat crossed the road because it was hungry." As a human, you instantly know "it" means the wombat, not the road! How does a Transformer figure that out?

Every token produces three short vectors.
First, a Query, Q: "What am I looking for?" For "it": "I'm a pronoun looking for a hungry creature!"
Second, a Key, K: "What label do I advertise to others?" "Wombat" advertises: "I'm an animal noun!"
Third, a Value, V: "What information will I hand over if you match with me?"

"It" compares its Query against every earlier word's Key; "wombat" wins the highest match score; and the Value of "wombat" flows right into "it", updating its meaning in context!

That clip used simplified numbers; what you're looking at now is 100 percent real attention extracted from Gemma 3, mathematically verified layer-by-layer against the model itself!

Inside Gemma 3, there are 18 layers, and each layer runs 4 Attention Heads side by side - 72 little spotlights in total, each learning its own specialist reading habit during training.

Right now, we've tuned into Layer 9, Head 2 on a classic linguistics test sentence: "beginning-of-sequence, The trophy did not fit in the suitcase because it was too big."

In English, "it" is ambiguous unless you understand physical size - what was too big, the trophy or the suitcase? Look at the purple arches rising out of yellow "it": 51 percent of Head 2's attention locks straight onto "trophy"! Only 8 percent goes to "suitcase". Without a single grammar rule programmed by a human, Layer 9 Head 2 evolved into a pronoun-resolver! Look at the purple grid on the bottom right, too - notice the dark black triangle in the upper-right half? That's called a causal mask: when generating text left-to-right, a word is only allowed to look at earlier words, never peek into the future!

Let's look at two other specialist heads Gemma grew on its own.

Here is Layer 2, Head 3: look at that crisp bright diagonal stripe in the heatmap! This head has one simple job: always look one word back - for instance, "it" puts 78 percent of its attention on "because" right before it - gluing adjacent word pairs together.

And here is a quirky discovery AI researchers call an Attention Sink, in Layer 17, Head 3. Because every row of percentages must add up to 100 percent, what does a spotlight do when there's nothing useful for it to look at in a sentence? It parks 95 percent of its attention harmlessly on beginning-of-sequence - the invisible marker at the very start of the line - using it as a mathematical parking lot!

Now zoom back and picture the entire Transformer assembled together. Look at that one-line recipe at the bottom of the card: Embeddings, into Attention plus a Neural Network Layer repeated N times, into Probabilities over the vocabulary.

Think of those two steps inside each layer as a study group.
First, Attention is when the words talk to each other across the table to gather context: "Hey, 'it' means 'trophy'!"
Second, the MLP - Multi-Layer Perceptron, the exact sigma of W x plus b neural network layers we built in Chapter 4 - is where each word sits quietly and processes what it just learned, recalling facts stored in its weights!

Stack that talk, process, talk, process block 18 times in tiny Gemma, or dozens of times in Gemini, and out the very top pops one final list of 262,144 raw scores - one score for every token in the dictionary!

Let's watch an 18-second clip of the final piece of maths that turns those raw scores into percentages: Softmax.

The raw scores coming out of the final layer - called logits - can be positive, negative, anything at all, and they don't add up to 100 percent.
So Softmax does two simple things.
First, raise e, 2.718, to the power of each score - making every number strictly positive and amplifying the leaders!
Second, divide each one by the grand total so all 262,144 bars sum to exactly 1, or 100 percent!

And remember our Temperature T slider from Slide 2? Look where orange T sits in the formula: it simply divides the raw scores right before exponentiating! Small T, like 0.3, exaggerates gaps so number one dominates; big T, like 2.5, shrinks gaps toward a uniform coin-toss!

Now we have come full circle back to Slide 2 - except nothing in that Softmax equation on the right is a mystery anymore!

Here is Gemma 3 270-million, pretrained, completing real science and geography sentences. For "The Great Barrier Reef is located off the coast of", it gives "Australia" 49 percent and "Queensland" 34 percent, continuing smoothly: "Australia, and is the largest coral reef system in the world..."

Ask "The chemical symbol for gold is", and at T equals 1.0, "A-u" leads at 32.4 percent ahead of "gold" at 23.2 percent. Watch what happens if I slide Temperature T down to 0.30.

"A-u" shoots straight up to 75 percent! That's why when you use AI for coding or factual science, low temperature keeps it focused, whereas for creative writing, higher temperature adds variety.

Now, watch closely, because this next step explains the single biggest trap students fall into when using AI for assignments! Let's ask this tiny 270-million-parameter raw model: "The largest planet in our solar system is..."

Look at the bars! Top choice: "the" at 15.8 percent, "a" at 11.0 percent, "home" at 3.6 percent, "Earth" at 2.8 percent. Where is "Jupiter"?! Buried way down in 25th place at 0.85 percent! And look at the yellow continuation at the bottom: "The largest planet in our solar system is the largest planet in the solar system. It is the largest planet in the solar system..."

You just witnessed a live hallucination and repetition loop! Why did that happen? Two crucial lessons.
First, a language model does not look up facts in a verified encyclopaedia; it predicts plausible-sounding next words! On the internet, sentences often continue "The largest planet in our solar system is home to the Great Red Spot...", so raw autocomplete happily picks "the" or "home"!
Second, notice I said this is the raw pre-trained model - it has only ever played "autocomplete the internet," not "be a helpful Q and A assistant"! How do you turn raw internet autocomplete into the helpful Gemini Web App? Next slide!

Training a frontier model like Gemini 4 actually happens in three distinct stages, shown on the left.

Stage one: Pre-training - learn the world. For weeks, thousands of TPUs play "predict the next token" across trillions of tokens of books, papers, code, images, and audio. Every time the model gives the true next word a low probability, cross-entropy Loss, negative log p, punishes it, and backpropagation nudges billions of weights. Out comes a brilliant - but unruly - internet autocompleter.

Stage two: Post-training - learn to be helpful and safe. Next, engineers fine-tune the model on curated Q and A examples, followed by Reinforcement Learning from Human and AI Feedback, RLHF - where raters score which of two answers is clearer, more honest, and safer. This is the stage that teaches Gemini to answer "Hi there! How can I help you?" instead of autocompleting your "hi" like a messy Reddit thread!

Stage three: Thinking - learn to reason. Finally, using Reinforcement Learning on problems where a computer can automatically check the final answer - like tough maths proofs and unit-tested code - the model is rewarded when generating step-by-step scratchpad reasoning leads to the right solution!

And on the right is one of the most astonishing graphs in modern science: the Chinchilla Scaling Law, discovered at DeepMind in 2022. As you spend more compute C, the model's error L drops along a remarkably smooth, predictable power law - provided you balance model size N, parameters, and training data D, tokens, at the green sweet spot of roughly 20 tokens of reading per parameter knob! Look at the orange dot for GPT-3: 175 billion knobs trained on only 300 billion tokens, about 1.7 tokens per knob - it was an oversized brain that hadn't read enough books! Chinchilla, with 70 billion knobs on 1.4 trillion tokens, used a smaller brain with more reading and beat GPT-3 soundly.

With a small budget of 10 to the 21st FLOPs, the law prescribes a roughly 2.8-billion-parameter model; and at frontier budgets of 3.2 times 10 to the 25th FLOPs, scaling both N and D drives the loss steadily, predictably lower - letting engineers forecast how smart a 100 million dollar training run will be before flipping the power switch!

Before we wrap up, let's be completely clear-eyed. Because you now know how the machine works - that at its core it is a giant, continuous probability engine - its flaws suddenly make total sense instead of seeming mysterious!

First, hallucination. As we saw live with "largest planet", an LLM optimises for plausible next tokens, not guaranteed truth. It can invent a fake court case or a non-existent journal D-O-I with 100 percent polite confidence. Never submit an unverified AI citation or calculation.

Second, bias and data. Models learn the statistical patterns of their training data - warts, stereotypes, over-represented languages, and scientific blind spots included.

Third, energy and cost. Multiplying compute 4 to 5 times every year costs real megawatts and hardware. Making models smaller, sparser like Mixture-of-Experts, and greener is one of the hottest research areas you could enter.

Fourth, evaluation. How do we even test how smart an AI is when standard exam questions accidentally leak into its internet training data? Building honest benchmarks is surprisingly hard.

Fifth, the black box. Ironically, even though we can inspect every single one of Gemma's 268,098,176 weights - just like we zoomed into them earlier! - explaining why millions of numbers conspired to reach a specific medical or legal decision remains an open frontier called mechanistic interpretability.

And sixth, safety and misuse. Preventing cyber-abuse, bio-risks, and deepfakes requires safety filters, frontier testing, and watermarking tools like Synth-ID baked into every release.

Here is the best rule of thumb for university: Treat AI like a brilliant, lightning-fast, occasionally overconfident lab partner who hasn't slept in three days. Brainstorm with it, ask it to quiz you, let it explain tough code - and always verify its work!
